# Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2026 — Conclusion & Key Takeaways

> **Source:** Prescott HC et al. *Surviving Sepsis Campaign: international guidelines for management of sepsis and septic shock 2026.* Intensive Care Med. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00134-026-08361-1

---

## Overview

The 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) guidelines represent the most current international consensus for managing sepsis and septic shock in adult patients. Developed by a 69-member multidisciplinary panel from 23 countries — with 38% of panelists practicing in LMICs — these guidelines contain **123 actionable statements** across 8 clinical domains, graded using the GRADE methodology.

Sepsis affects approximately **49 million people** globally each year, causing **13 million deaths**. The 2026 update builds upon the 2021 guidelines with new, revised, and carryover recommendations, incorporating principles of antimicrobial stewardship, equity, and patient-centered care.

## Domain-by-Domain Conclusions

### 1. Screening & Early Management
Early recognition is the cornerstone of sepsis survival. Hospitals should implement structured **performance improvement programs** including sepsis screening, standard operating procedures, and quality improvement strategies. **NEWS/NEWS2/MEWS/SIRS** are recommended over qSOFA for in-hospital screening. "Code sepsis" or "sepsis huddle" multidisciplinary team protocols are suggested to expedite diagnosis and treatment. Sepsis is a clinical diagnosis — no single biomarker can rule it in or out. Blood cultures should be collected **before** antimicrobial therapy whenever possible. ICU admission within 6 hours is suggested when indicated.

### 2. Infection Management
Antimicrobial therapy remains the most effective initial intervention. For septic shock and probable/definite sepsis, antibiotics should be initiated **ideally within 1 hour** of recognition. Empirical therapy should cover the most likely pathogens based on infection source, local epidemiology, and MDR risk factors. Antifungal therapy is suggested in high-risk patients. **Prolonged beta-lactam infusion** (after an initial bolus dose) is recommended for maintenance therapy. Daily reassessment for **de-escalation** is essential — stopping or narrowing antimicrobials when no longer indicated reduces resistance and adverse effects. Antimicrobial therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) should be used selectively in appropriate patients.

### 3. Hemodynamic Management
Fluid resuscitation with **≥30 mL/kg IV crystalloid** within the first 3 hours remains conditionally recommended for sepsis-induced hypoperfusion; however, individualization and ongoing clinical reassessment are emphasized. **Balanced crystalloids** are preferred over 0.9% normal saline for ongoing resuscitation. Starches and gelatin are not recommended. **Norepinephrine** is the first-line vasopressor of choice. Peripheral vasopressor initiation (rather than waiting for central access) is now conditionally suggested. The MAP target of **65 mmHg** is maintained; a lower target of **60–65 mmHg** may be considered in patients ≥65 years old. Vasopressin can be added in refractory hypotension. Corticosteroids (IV hydrocortisone) are suggested for patients with ongoing vasopressor requirements.

### 4. Respiratory Support
Oxygenation monitoring with SpO₂ or arterial blood gas is standard. **High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC)** is preferred over conventional oxygen therapy for acute hypoxemic respiratory failure. For patients with ARDS, **low tidal volume ventilation (6 mL/kg IBW)** and plateau pressure ≤30 cmH₂O are strongly recommended. Prone positioning for **>12 hours/day** in moderate-severe ARDS improves outcomes. An incremental PEEP strategy is now recommended **against** in moderate-severe ARDS. Intermittent NMBA boluses are preferred over continuous infusion. VV-ECMO may be used in experienced centers when conventional mechanical ventilation fails.

### 5. Adjunctive & Additional Therapies
Several previously used therapies have been de-emphasized or recommended against: **IV Vitamin C**, intravenous immunoglobulins, blood purification therapies (hemoperfusion, high-dose hemofiltration, plasma exchange), antipyretic therapy for fever alone, Vitamin D therapy for sepsis treatment, and probiotics are all now **suggested against**. Stress ulcer prophylaxis with PPIs remains recommended for patients with GI bleeding risk factors. **Restrictive transfusion strategy** (targeting Hb ≥70 g/L) is recommended. VTE prophylaxis with **low molecular weight heparin** is strongly recommended. Insulin therapy targeting glucose **≤180 mg/dL** (10 mmol/L) is recommended. Early **enteral nutrition** within 72 hours is suggested.

### 6. Goals of Care
Clinicians must proactively discuss goals of care with patients and families. It is suggested to address goals of care **early (within 72 hours)** rather than late. While palliative care principles should be integrated into the treatment plan, routine formal palliative care consultation for all patients is **not** recommended. Time-limited trials (TLT) of critical care are a useful tool when prognosis or patient values are uncertain.

### 7. Transitions of Care
Patients with sepsis face significant risks at care transitions. Critical care transition programs and structured **handoff processes** are suggested. Comprehensive **medication reconciliation** (ideally pharmacist-led) should occur at all transitions. Clinicians should screen for economic, nutritional, financial, and social support needs at discharge. Patients and families should receive written and verbal sepsis education — diagnosis, treatment course, common impairments, and post-ICU syndrome — before and after discharge.

### 8. Long-term Outcomes & Recovery
Post-sepsis syndrome is common, encompassing physical, cognitive, and mental health impairments. Survivors should be offered **post-critical illness follow-up services**, including physical rehabilitation (especially after prolonged mechanical ventilation), and **mental health support**. Health systems should facilitate proactive assessment and referral for these needs. Research into cognition-targeted therapies remains insufficient to issue formal recommendations.

## Overall Conclusion

The SSC 2026 guidelines reinforce the principle that **time-sensitive, protocol-driven care saves lives in sepsis**. The most impactful interventions remain early recognition, rapid antimicrobial therapy, appropriate fluid resuscitation, and early vasopressor use when needed. Equally important are the principles of stewardship — avoiding harm from unnecessary antibiotics, fluids, and adjunctive treatments — and the emerging recognition that **sepsis survivorship** requires sustained, structured support well beyond hospital discharge.

Key shifts from 2021 include:
- Stronger emphasis on **quality improvement programs** and code sepsis protocols
- **NEWS/NEWS2** favored over qSOFA for in-hospital screening
- Peripheral vasopressors now conditionally **suggested early**, before central access is secured
- Lower MAP target **(60–65 mmHg)** conditionally suggested for elderly patients
- **Prolonged beta-lactam infusion** now recommended for maintenance
- Several adjunctive therapies (Vitamin C, IVIG, blood purification) are now formally **recommended against**
- Robust new sections on **transitions of care** and **long-term recovery**

These guidelines empower clinicians globally to deliver evidence-based, equitable, and patient-centered sepsis care — from the ambulance to post-hospital recovery.
